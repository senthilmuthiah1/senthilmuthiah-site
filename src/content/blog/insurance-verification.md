## What does "verify insurance" mean?

Before a patient sees the doctor, someone in the office checks their insurance. They want to know two simple things:

1. **Will the insurance company pay for this visit?**
2. **How much will the patient have to pay?**

This check is called **insurance verification** (you'll also hear **eligibility check** or **benefits check**).

Think of it like booking a hotel. Before you travel, you confirm the booking is real, the room is paid for, and what extras you'll pay at the desk. You don't want to find out at midnight that there's no room. Insurance verification is the same — you find out *before* the visit, not weeks later when the bill is refused.

## Why it matters so much

If the office skips this step, many things can go wrong:

- The patient's insurance **ended last month** — the insurance company pays nothing.
- The doctor is **not in the patient's network** — the patient pays much more than expected, and is unhappy.
- The visit **needed approval in advance** — the insurance company refuses to pay, and the office usually can't charge the patient either.

In every case, the office finds out **weeks later**, when the claim comes back unpaid. By then, fixing it means phone calls, paperwork, and sometimes money that's never collected.

**A five-minute check before the visit prevents most of this.** That's why experienced billing people say insurance verification is the most important step in the whole billing process.

(New to words like *copay* and *in-network*? Read [US Health Insurance Basics](/blog/health-insurance-basics/) first.)

## When is it done?

- **A day or two before the visit** — for scheduled appointments. This gives time to fix problems.
- **Again at check-in** — insurance can change overnight.
- **At every visit** — even for patients who come every month. People change jobs and plans and often forget to tell the doctor's office.

## How is it done?

There are three ways, from fastest to slowest:

| Way | How it works | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **In the office software** | Click a button; the software asks the insurance company and shows the answer | Seconds |
| **On the insurer's website** | Log in to the insurance company's website for doctors and look up the patient | A few minutes |
| **By phone** | Call the insurance company's number for doctors (it's on the back of the card) | 10–30 minutes |

Most offices use the software first, and only phone when the answer is unclear or missing.

> **Technical name:** the software sends a standard electronic question called a **270** and gets back an answer called a **271**. Every insurance company in the US must accept them.

## The eight questions to ask

Here is a sample verification for **Anna**, who has a visit with **Dr. Lee** tomorrow. (All details are made up.)

![A sample insurance verification sheet with eight questions answered for patient Anna Smith](/images/posts/insurance-verification-sheet.svg)

Let's go through each question in plain words.

### 1. Is the insurance active today?

Is Anna still covered on the **date of the visit**? Also note the **start date** (when the plan began) and any **end date**. A card in someone's wallet is not proof — it could be last year's.

### 2. What type of plan is it?

HMO, PPO, EPO, Medicare, Medicaid…? The type tells you the rules. For example, an HMO usually needs a **referral** from the patient's main doctor before they can see a specialist. (See [Types of US Health Insurance](/blog/types-of-health-insurance/).)

### 3. Is our doctor in-network?

If Dr. Lee has a contract with BlueHealth, she's **in-network**, and Anna pays less. If not, Anna may pay much more — or the plan may not pay at all. Always check for the **specific doctor**, not just the clinic.

### 4. What is the copay?

The fixed amount Anna pays for this kind of visit — here **$25**. It's often different for a regular doctor, a specialist, or an emergency room. The front desk collects it at check-in.

### 5. How much of the deductible is left?

The **deductible** is the amount Anna must pay each year before her insurance starts sharing costs. Her deductible is $1,000, and she still has **$400 left** to pay this year.

For Anna's simple office visit, her plan charges a copay instead, so the deductible doesn't apply. But if she needed something bigger — like an MRI scan — she would pay the first $400 herself. That's why it's marked **"!"** on the sheet: it's worth telling her.

### 6. What is the coinsurance?

After the deductible is used up, Anna pays a **percentage** of the cost — here **20%** — and BlueHealth pays the rest.

### 7. Does this visit need approval in advance?

Some services — surgery, scans, some medicines — need the insurance company's **approval before** they're done. This is called **prior authorization**. Anna's office visit doesn't need it. If it did, the office would request it now, before the visit.

### 8. Does the patient have any other insurance?

If yes, which one pays **first**? Anna also has **StarCare** through her husband, so BlueHealth is billed first and StarCare second. (See [Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Insurance](/blog/primary-secondary-tertiary-insurance/).) That's the second **"!"** on the sheet.

## If you have to phone

Calls to insurance companies follow a pattern. Here's a simple example:

> **Office:** Hi, I'm calling from Lee Family Clinic to check a patient's benefits.
>
> **Insurer:** Can I have the doctor's NPI number and the patient's member ID?
>
> **Office:** The NPI is 1234567893. The patient is Anna Smith, member ID BHX482913557, date of birth 12 March 1990.
>
> **Insurer:** Thank you. Anna's plan is active, it's a PPO, and the doctor is in-network.
>
> **Office:** What's her copay for an office visit, and how much deductible is left?
>
> **Insurer:** $25 copay. $400 left on a $1,000 deductible.
>
> **Office:** Does an office visit need prior authorization?
>
> **Insurer:** No.
>
> **Office:** Thank you. Can I have your name and a reference number for this call?

**Always write down** the name of the person you spoke to, the date and time, and the **call reference number**. If the insurance company later refuses to pay, this record proves what you were told.

## What to do with the answers

- **Save them** in the patient's file in the office software.
- **Tell the patient** what they'll pay — before the visit, not after.
- **Collect the copay** at check-in.
- **Request approval** if anything needs prior authorization.
- **Update the insurance order** if there's a second plan.
- **Fix the patient's details** if anything doesn't match — name spelling, date of birth, member ID.

If the insurance is **not active**, tell the patient before the visit. They can bring new insurance details, reschedule, or agree to pay themselves (**self-pay**).

## The one thing to remember

**Check before the visit, every visit.** Most unpaid claims can be traced back to a question nobody asked at the front desk.

## Words you'll hear

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Insurance verification** | Checking a patient's insurance before the visit |
| **Eligibility** | Whether the patient is covered on a given date |
| **Benefits** | What the plan pays for, and what the patient pays |
| **Effective date** | The date the insurance started |
| **Prior authorization** | The insurance company's approval, needed before some services |
| **Referral** | Permission from the patient's main doctor to see a specialist |
| **Call reference number** | A number the insurer gives to identify a phone call |
| **Self-pay** | The patient pays for the visit themselves |
