*Part of my beginner series on US medical billing. No healthcare background needed.*

If you're new to US healthcare — maybe you're joining a billing company, or building software for one — the first thing to understand isn't billing. It's **insurance**. Almost every billing step depends on what the patient's insurance will pay, and what the patient must pay themselves.

This post covers the basic words. Once you know them, the [medical billing process](/blog/medical-billing-process/) will make much more sense.

## What is health insurance?

Health insurance is an agreement: a person pays a regular fee, and in return the insurance company pays a large part of their medical bills.

In the US, most people get insurance through their **employer**. Others get it from the government (for example **Medicare** for people aged 65 and over), or buy it themselves. We'll look at all the types in a [later post](/blog/types-of-health-insurance/).

A few names you'll hear for the same thing:

- **Insurance company = insurer = payer = carrier.** "Payer" is the word billing people use most, because it's the one who pays.
- **Patient = member = insured = subscriber.** The *subscriber* is the person who holds the policy. If Anna is covered through her husband's job, her husband is the subscriber and Anna is a *dependent*.

## Who pays what?

The patient doesn't pay nothing — they pay their share in several ways. Let's say Anna has a BlueHealth plan.

| Word | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Premium** | The monthly fee to keep the insurance active, paid whether or not you see a doctor | $300 a month, often partly paid by the employer |
| **Deductible** | The amount the patient pays each year *before* insurance starts paying | The first $1,000 of bills each year |
| **Copay** | A fixed amount paid at each visit | $25 for a doctor visit |
| **Coinsurance** | A percentage the patient pays *after* the deductible | Insurance pays 80%, Anna pays 20% |
| **Out-of-pocket maximum** | The most the patient pays in a year; after that, insurance pays 100% | $5,000 a year |

Not every plan has all of these. Some plans have only copays; others have a deductible and coinsurance. That's why billing staff check the patient's plan before each visit.

**For billing, the premium doesn't matter** — it's between the patient and the insurer. The deductible, copay and coinsurance matter a lot, because they decide how much the office collects from the patient and how much from the insurer.

## In-network or out-of-network?

Insurance companies sign **contracts** with doctors and hospitals. A provider with a contract is **in-network**, also called **participating** or **"par"**. A provider without one is **out-of-network** or **"non-par"**.

| | In-network (par) | Out-of-network (non-par) |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Accepts the insurer's agreed price (the *allowed amount*) | Not bound by the insurer's price |
| **Who gets paid** | Insurer usually pays the provider directly | Insurer often pays the patient, who then pays the provider |
| **Patient pays** | Less | More — and some plans pay nothing at all |
| **Rules** | Provider must follow the insurer's rules | Fewer rules, but harder to collect |

This matters because an in-network provider **can't bill the patient** for the difference between their full price and the allowed amount. If Dr. Lee charges $150 and BlueHealth's allowed amount is $100, the extra $50 is simply written off.

In-network providers must also follow the insurer's rules. For example, if a service needs **approval in advance** (a *prior authorization*) and the office didn't get it, the insurer won't pay — and under the contract, the office usually can't charge the patient either. A mistake at the front desk becomes money lost.

## How to read an insurance card

Insured patients carry an insurance card, usually the size of a credit card. Here's a made-up example:

![A sample health insurance card, front and back, with six numbered parts](/images/posts/insurance-card.svg)

1. **Member name** — the person covered.
2. **Member ID** — the patient's unique number with this insurer. It goes on every claim. One wrong letter and the claim is rejected.
3. **Group number** — identifies the employer's plan. Not every card has one.
4. **Copays** — what the patient pays at each type of visit.
5. **Claims address** — where to mail paper claims.
6. **Payer ID** — a short code that tells the clearinghouse which insurer to send an electronic claim to.

The card may also show the plan type (HMO, PPO and so on), the effective date, and the name of the patient's main doctor.

### Card tips for the front desk

- **Copy both sides** of the card for the patient's file.
- **Ask for the card at every visit.** Patients change jobs and plans and often forget to tell the doctor's office. Sometimes the insurer quietly changes the member ID format, and the patient doesn't notice.
- **A card is not proof of coverage.** The patient can still have last year's card. Always check eligibility electronically or with the insurer.
- **Some patients have two cards** — for example, a Medicare card and a separate card from a private Medicare plan that replaces it. The billing team needs to know which one to bill.

## The one thing to remember

**Know the patient's plan before the visit.** Is the insurance active? Is the provider in-network? What does the patient owe today? Getting these three answers right at the front desk prevents most billing problems later.

## Words you'll hear

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Payer** | The insurance company |
| **Subscriber** | The person who holds the insurance policy |
| **Dependent** | A family member covered under the subscriber's policy |
| **Allowed amount** | The price the insurer agrees to pay for a service |
| **Par / non-par** | In-network / out-of-network |
| **Prior authorization** | Insurer's approval needed before some services |
| **Eligibility** | Whether the patient's insurance is active and what it covers |
