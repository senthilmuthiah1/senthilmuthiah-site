*Part of my beginner series on US medical billing. No healthcare background needed.*

Unlike many countries, the US doesn't have one national health insurance. It has **many different types**, and each one has its own rules: who can see which doctor, what the patient pays, and where the claim goes.

For a billing person, the insurance type answers three questions:

1. **Where** do we send the claim?
2. **What rules** must we follow to get paid (referrals, approvals, deadlines)?
3. **How much** does the patient owe?

New to words like *deductible* and *in-network*? Read [US Health Insurance Basics](/blog/health-insurance-basics/) first.

## 1. Private ("commercial") insurance

Insurance sold by private companies is called **commercial insurance**. Most Americans get it through their **employer**, who pays part of the cost. People without an employer plan can buy one themselves on the **ACA marketplace** (often called "Obamacare" plans).

Commercial plans come in a few styles. The main difference is **how free the patient is to choose a doctor**.

### HMO — Health Maintenance Organization

- The patient picks one main doctor, the **primary care physician (PCP)**.
- To see a specialist, the patient needs a **referral** from the PCP.
- Only **in-network** doctors are covered. Outside the network, the patient pays everything (except emergencies).
- Usually the cheapest for the patient — often just a small copay per visit.

### PPO — Preferred Provider Organization

- No PCP needed. The patient can see specialists directly.
- In-network doctors cost less; **out-of-network** doctors are covered too, but the patient pays more.
- More freedom, higher premium.

### EPO — Exclusive Provider Organization

- No PCP and usually no referrals — like a PPO.
- But only **in-network** doctors are covered — like an HMO.

### Indemnity — the old style

- The patient can see **any** doctor.
- After the deductible, the insurer pays a percentage (often 80%) and the patient pays the rest.
- Rare today, but you'll still see it.

### Side by side

| | HMO | PPO | EPO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Needs a main doctor (PCP)? | Yes | No | No |
| Needs referrals for specialists? | Yes | Usually no | Usually no |
| Out-of-network covered? | No | Yes, at a higher cost | No |
| Cost to the patient | Lowest | Highest | In between |

**Why billing cares:** with an HMO, a missing referral or a non-network doctor means the claim is **denied** — and the office often can't bill the patient either. The front desk must check these rules *before* the visit.

## 2. Government insurance

### Medicare — for people 65 and over

Medicare is the **federal** government's insurance for people aged **65 and over**, and for some younger people with disabilities or permanent kidney failure. It has four parts:

| Part | Covers | In simple words |
|---|---|---|
| **Part A** | Hospital stays, nursing facilities, hospice | "Hospital insurance" — billed on the UB-04 |
| **Part B** | Doctor visits, outpatient care, tests | "Medical insurance" — billed on the CMS-1500 |
| **Part C** | Medicare Advantage plans | A *private* insurer runs Medicare for the patient, often as an HMO or PPO |
| **Part D** | Prescription medicines | Usually doesn't affect a doctor's billing |

Things a beginner should know:

- With **original Medicare** (Parts A and B), the patient pays a yearly deductible, then Medicare usually pays **80%** of the allowed amount. Many patients buy a **supplement** plan to cover the other 20%.
- With a **Medicare Advantage** (Part C) plan, the claim goes to **the private plan, not to Medicare**. The patient may carry both a Medicare card and the plan's card — the plan's card is the one to bill.
- Medicare cards use a **Medicare Beneficiary Identifier (MBI)** — an 11-character ID. Older cards used Social Security numbers, which are no longer accepted.

### Medicaid and CHIP — for people with low income

- **Medicaid** helps people and families with **low income**. It's paid for by both the federal government and the states, but **each state runs its own program**, so the rules differ from state to state.
- **CHIP** (Children's Health Insurance Program) covers children in families that earn too much for Medicaid but can't afford private insurance.
- In most states, many Medicaid patients are in **Medicaid managed care plans** run by private companies — again, two cards, and you bill the plan.
- Medicaid is usually the **payer of last resort**: if the patient has any other insurance, that is billed first.

### TRICARE and VA — for the military

- **TRICARE** covers active-duty military members, retirees and their families.
- The **VA** (Department of Veterans Affairs) provides care for veterans.

## 3. Injury-related insurance

Some insurance pays only for **one specific injury** — not for the patient's everyday care.

### Workers' compensation

If someone is hurt **at work**, their **employer's workers' comp insurance** pays — not their regular health insurance. Each state has its own workers' comp rules and fee schedules.

The billing team needs the **date of injury**, the **employer**, and the **workers' comp claim number**. Billing a work injury to the patient's regular insurance is a common mistake — and it can even hurt the patient's legal case.

### Auto and no-fault insurance

If someone is hurt in a **car accident**, the **car insurance** may pay the medical bills. On the CMS-1500 claim form, a box marks the visit as related to an auto accident. Some auto insurers also want medical notes or approval in advance.

## When a patient has more than one insurance

Many patients have **two or even three** insurances — for example, Medicare plus a supplement, or their own employer plan plus their spouse's. One is billed first (**primary**), and whatever is left goes to the next (**secondary**, then **tertiary**). Deciding the order is called **coordination of benefits**, and getting it wrong leads to denials. It's a big topic, so it gets its own post later in this series.

## The one thing to remember

**The insurance type decides the rules.** Before every visit, the office should know: Which plan is this? Is our doctor in its network? Does the visit need a referral or approval? Answer these up front, and most denials never happen.

## Words you'll hear

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Commercial insurance** | Private insurance, usually through an employer |
| **PCP** | Primary care physician — the patient's main doctor |
| **Referral** | Permission from the PCP to see a specialist |
| **Medicare Advantage** | Part C — a private plan that replaces original Medicare |
| **Supplement (Medigap)** | A plan that pays what original Medicare doesn't |
| **MBI** | The patient's Medicare ID number |
| **Payer of last resort** | Pays only after all other insurance (Medicaid) |
| **Coordination of benefits** | Deciding which insurance pays first |
