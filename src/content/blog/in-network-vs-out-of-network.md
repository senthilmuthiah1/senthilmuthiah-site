*Part of my beginner series on US medical billing. No healthcare background needed.*

We met **in-network** and **out-of-network** briefly in [US Health Insurance Basics](/blog/health-insurance-basics/). This post goes deeper, because the difference decides **how much** a patient pays, **who** gets paid, and **what** the billing team is allowed to bill.

## The simple idea

Think of a mobile phone plan. Calls to numbers **inside** your network are cheap or free. Calls **outside** it cost much more. Nothing is stopping you from calling outside — it just costs more.

Health insurance works the same way:

- **In-network** — the doctor or hospital has a **contract** with the patient's insurance company.
- **Out-of-network** — there's **no contract**.

The contract is what changes everything.

## What the contract means

When Dr. Lee signs a contract with BlueHealth, both sides agree to rules:

| Dr. Lee agrees to… | BlueHealth agrees to… |
|---|---|
| Accept BlueHealth's **agreed price** (the *allowed amount*) | Send her patients — she's listed in its directory |
| **Not** bill patients more than their copay, deductible and coinsurance | Pay her **directly** |
| Follow its rules — prior authorization, filing deadlines and so on | Pay within set time limits |

You'll also hear **participating** or **"par"** for in-network, and **non-participating** or **"non-par"** for out-of-network.

## The same visit, two very different bills

Anna sees a dermatologist. The doctor charges **$300**.

![The same $300 visit: Anna pays $30 in-network, but $210 out-of-network because of the balance bill](/images/posts/in-network-vs-out-of-network.svg)

### If the dermatologist is in-network

- BlueHealth's agreed price is **$150**. The other **$150** is written off by the doctor — under the contract, Anna can't be billed for it.
- After her deductible, Anna pays **20%** coinsurance: **$30**.
- BlueHealth pays **$120**.
- **Anna pays $30.**

### If the dermatologist is out-of-network

Anna's PPO plan does cover out-of-network care — but less generously.

- BlueHealth still decides its own **allowed amount**: **$150**.
- It pays only **60%** out-of-network: **$90**. Anna's coinsurance is **40%**: **$60**.
- There's no contract, so the doctor doesn't have to write anything off. The doctor can bill Anna for the remaining **$150**. This is called a **balance bill**.
- **Anna pays $210.**

Same visit, same charge — **seven times** more for the patient.

## Balance billing

**Balance billing** means an out-of-network provider billing the patient for the difference between their **full charge** and what the insurer **allowed**.

- **In-network** providers **can't** balance bill. It's in their contract.
- **Out-of-network** providers usually **can** — except where the law protects the patient.

## Does the plan pay anything out-of-network?

It depends on the plan type. (See [Types of US Health Insurance](/blog/types-of-health-insurance/).)

| Plan | Out-of-network care (not an emergency) |
|---|---|
| **HMO** | Usually **not covered** — the patient pays everything |
| **EPO** | Usually **not covered** |
| **PPO** | **Covered**, but with a higher deductible and coinsurance — and possible balance billing |
| **Original Medicare** | Most doctors accept Medicare; those who don't accept its price can charge only a limited amount more |

Some plans make exceptions — for example, when there's **no in-network specialist** nearby. The patient or doctor usually has to ask for approval first.

## Who gets paid?

- **In-network:** the insurer pays the **provider** directly.
- **Out-of-network:** the insurer often pays the **patient**, who then pays the provider. Some out-of-network providers send the claim for the patient; others ask the patient to pay upfront and claim the money back themselves.

A patient can sign an **assignment of benefits** form, which asks the insurer to pay the provider directly instead of the patient. (On the CMS-1500, this is the patient's signature in box 13.)

## Surprise bills — and the law that stops them

Imagine this:

> **Raj** has surgery at **City Hospital**, which is **in-network** with his insurance. He chose the hospital and the surgeon carefully. But the **anesthesiologist** on duty that day is **out-of-network** — and Raj had no say in who it would be. Weeks later, Raj gets a big balance bill from the anesthesiologist.

That's a **surprise bill**: an out-of-network charge the patient couldn't reasonably avoid. For years, it was a common and painful problem in the US.

### The No Surprises Act

Since **1 January 2022**, a federal law called the **No Surprises Act** protects patients in situations like Raj's. In simple terms:

- **Emergency care** — including at an out-of-network emergency room — can't be balance billed. The patient pays only what they'd pay **in-network**.
- **Out-of-network doctors at an in-network hospital or facility** — like Raj's anesthesiologist, or a radiologist or assistant surgeon — can't balance bill for that care. The patient pays only **in-network** cost sharing.
- **Air ambulances** are covered too.
- **Disputes about price are between the provider and the insurer**, not the patient. They can use a formal process called **independent dispute resolution**.
- **Uninsured or self-pay patients** have the right to a **good faith estimate** of the cost before scheduled care.

Some limits beginners should know:

- **Ground ambulances** aren't covered by the federal law (some states have their own rules).
- For some **planned** care, an out-of-network provider can ask the patient to **agree in writing**, in advance, to be billed out-of-network. This isn't allowed for emergency care or for certain services the patient can't choose, like anesthesia or radiology.
- Many **states** have their own protections too.

**For the billing team:** before sending any out-of-network bill to a patient, check whether the visit is protected. Balance billing a protected patient can lead to penalties.

## Questions the front desk should ask

- Is **this specific doctor** in-network — not just the clinic or hospital?
- Is the network the one for **this patient's plan**? An insurer can have several networks.
- Does the plan cover **out-of-network** care at all?
- If not in-network — does the patient know, and do they agree to the cost?

The insurer's online **provider directory** helps, but directories aren't always up to date. When in doubt, **ask the insurer**. (See [Insurance Verification](/blog/insurance-verification/).)

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming the clinic's network status applies to every doctor** in it.
- **Balance billing an in-network patient** — not allowed under the contract.
- **Balance billing a patient protected by the No Surprises Act.**
- **Not telling patients** they're seeing an out-of-network provider before planned care.
- **New doctors** billed as in-network before their contract is active. (See [NPI, Tax ID and Other Provider Numbers](/blog/npi-tax-id-provider-numbers/) on enrollment.)

## The one thing to remember

**In-network means a contract: the agreed price, no balance billing. Out-of-network means no contract: the patient pays more — unless the No Surprises Act protects them.**

## Words you'll hear

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **In-network / participating (par)** | Has a contract with the insurer |
| **Out-of-network / non-par** | Has no contract with the insurer |
| **Allowed amount** | The most the insurer will consider for a service |
| **Balance billing** | Billing the patient for the difference between the charge and the allowed amount |
| **Surprise bill** | An unexpected out-of-network bill the patient couldn't reasonably avoid |
| **No Surprises Act** | The 2022 federal law that protects patients from many surprise bills |
| **Independent dispute resolution** | The process providers and insurers use to settle price disputes under the law |
| **Good faith estimate** | A written cost estimate for uninsured or self-pay patients |
| **Assignment of benefits** | A patient's permission for the insurer to pay the provider directly |
| **Provider directory** | The insurer's list of in-network providers |
