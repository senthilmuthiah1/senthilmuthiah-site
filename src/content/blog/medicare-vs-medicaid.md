*Part of my beginner series on US medical billing. No healthcare background needed.*

**Medicare** and **Medicaid** are the two biggest government health programs in the US. Their names sound almost the same, both are run by the government, and many patients have both. No wonder new billers mix them up.

But they are completely different programs, with different rules, different ID numbers and different places to send the claim. Mixing them up is one of the fastest ways to get a claim rejected.

Here's an easy way to remember them:

- **Medi-CARE** — *care* for the **elderly**. Based mainly on **age**.
- **Medi-CAID** — *aid* for people with **low income**. Based mainly on **income**.

(New to *deductible*, *premium* and *allowed amount*? Read [US Health Insurance Basics](/blog/health-insurance-basics/) first. For the full list of insurance types, see [Types of US Health Insurance](/blog/types-of-health-insurance/).)

## Medicare vs Medicaid at a glance

| | **Medicare** | **Medicaid** |
|---|---|---|
| **Who it's for** | People **65 and over**, and some younger people with disabilities | People with **low income** — children, pregnant women, some adults, older and disabled people |
| **Based on** | Age (or disability), **not income** | Income (and sometimes assets) |
| **Who runs it** | The **federal** government — same rules nationwide | Each **state**, within federal rules — rules differ by state |
| **Who pays for it** | Federal government, plus premiums from patients | Federal government **and** the state, together |
| **Cost to the patient** | Premiums, deductibles and coinsurance | Usually little or nothing |
| **Patient's ID** | Medicare Beneficiary Identifier (MBI) | A state Medicaid ID number |
| **Bill order** | Usually primary for retired patients | Almost always **last** (payer of last resort) |

Now let's look at each one properly.

## Part 1 — Medicare

### Who can get Medicare?

Medicare is the federal government's health insurance for:

- people aged **65 and over** — whatever their income. A retired millionaire gets Medicare just like a retired factory worker.
- people **under 65** who have received Social Security disability benefits for **24 months**
- people of any age with **permanent kidney failure** who need dialysis or a kidney transplant
- people with **ALS** (Lou Gehrig's disease)

Think of it like a **pension**: people pay Medicare taxes from their salary throughout their working life, and receive the benefit when they retire.

> **Technical name:** Medicare is run by **CMS** — the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. Claims are processed by private companies hired by CMS for each region, called **Medicare Administrative Contractors (MACs)**.

### The four parts of Medicare

Medicare is split into four "parts." Think of them like the sections of a **combo meal** — each part covers a different kind of care, and the patient can have some parts and not others.

#### Part A — Hospital insurance

Covers care when the patient is **admitted**:

- hospital stays (inpatient)
- a short stay in a **skilled nursing facility** after a hospital stay
- **hospice** care for people near the end of life
- some home health care

Most people pay **no monthly premium** for Part A, because they (or their spouse) paid Medicare taxes for about 10 years while working. They still pay a **deductible** for each hospital stay.

Hospitals bill Part A on the **UB-04** form (see [CMS-1500 vs UB-04](/blog/cms-1500-vs-ub-04/)).

#### Part B — Medical insurance

Covers care **outside a hospital stay**:

- **doctor visits** — in the clinic or in the hospital
- outpatient care and surgery
- lab tests, X-rays and scans
- preventive care, such as screenings and flu shots
- medical equipment like wheelchairs and walkers (called **DME**)

Everyone pays a **monthly premium** for Part B. After a yearly deductible, Medicare usually pays **80%** of the allowed amount and the patient pays the other **20%** (coinsurance — see [Deductible, Copay and Coinsurance](/blog/deductible-copay-coinsurance/)).

Most doctors bill Part B on the **CMS-1500** form.

> **Technical name:** Part A claims are sent electronically as the **837I** (institutional) and Part B claims as the **837P** (professional). Both go to the MAC for that region.

#### Together, Parts A and B are "original Medicare"

Parts A and B together are called **original Medicare** (or "traditional Medicare"). The government pays the bills directly, and the patient can see **any doctor in the country who accepts Medicare** — no network, no referrals.

The catch: original Medicare has **no yearly limit** on what the patient pays. That 20% can add up to a lot. So many patients buy a **Medicare Supplement plan** (also called **Medigap**) from a private insurer to pay that 20% and the deductibles.

#### Part C — Medicare Advantage

This is the part that confuses beginners most.

**Part C is not extra coverage.** It's a **different way to get** Parts A and B. Instead of the government, a **private insurance company** (like UnitedHealthcare, Humana or Aetna) provides the patient's Medicare benefits. The government pays that company a fixed amount every month for each patient.

Medicare Advantage plans:

- usually work like an **HMO or PPO** — with a network, and sometimes referrals and prior authorizations
- have a **yearly limit** on what the patient pays
- often include extras — dental, vision, hearing, gym memberships — and usually Part D drug coverage too

They are very popular: more than half of everyone with Medicare is now in a Medicare Advantage plan.

**For billing, this is the key point:** when a patient has Medicare Advantage, you bill **the private plan, not Medicare.** If you send the claim to Medicare, it will be rejected.

#### Part D — Prescription drug coverage

Covers **medicines** the patient buys at the pharmacy. It's sold by private insurers, either on its own (with original Medicare) or built into a Medicare Advantage plan.

Part D is billed by **pharmacies**, so it rarely affects a doctor's or hospital's billing.

### Original Medicare vs Medicare Advantage

| | **Original Medicare (A + B)** | **Medicare Advantage (Part C)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Who pays the claim** | The government (through the MAC) | A private insurance company |
| **Which doctors** | Any doctor that accepts Medicare | Usually only the plan's network |
| **Referrals / prior auth** | Rarely needed | Often needed |
| **Yearly limit on patient costs** | No (unless they buy Medigap) | Yes |
| **Drug coverage** | Separate Part D plan | Usually included |
| **Card to bill** | The red, white and blue Medicare card | The **plan's own card** |

A Medicare Advantage patient often still carries their red, white and blue Medicare card. At check-in, always ask: **"Do you have a Medicare Advantage plan, or any other card?"**

## Part 2 — Medicaid

### Who can get Medicaid?

Medicaid helps people who **can't afford** health care. Groups usually covered include:

- children in low-income families
- pregnant women
- low-income adults — in states that chose to **expand** Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act (ACA)
- low-income people who are **older or disabled**
- people who need **long-term nursing home care** and have used up their savings

That last one is important. **Medicare does not pay for long-term nursing home care** (only short stays after a hospital visit). Medicaid does — so it's the biggest payer for nursing homes in the US.

### Every state runs its own Medicaid

The federal government sets the basic rules and pays a large share of the cost. But **each state runs its own program** — like the **franchises** of a restaurant chain: same brand, but each franchise sets its own menu and prices.

So, from state to state, these all differ:

- **who qualifies** and the income limits
- **what is covered** (dental, therapy, transport and more)
- the **program name** — for example Medi-Cal in California, MassHealth in Massachusetts
- **prior authorization** rules and **claim deadlines**

A biller who knows Texas Medicaid well can't assume Florida Medicaid works the same way.

### Medicaid managed care

In most states, many Medicaid patients don't use the state's own plan. They are placed in a **Medicaid managed care plan** run by a private insurer, which works much like an HMO.

Just like Medicare Advantage: **bill the managed care plan, not the state.** The patient may have a state Medicaid card **and** the plan's card.

### Medicaid eligibility can change quickly

A Medicare patient stays on Medicare for life. A Medicaid patient's eligibility can **end** if their income goes up, if they miss their renewal paperwork, or — from 2027 — if they don't meet new **work requirements**. Under a 2025 federal law, states must start work requirements for many adults in the expansion group by January 1, 2027, and check eligibility at least every six months.

**For billing:** check Medicaid eligibility **before every visit**, not just at the first one. "She had Medicaid last month" is not proof she has it today.

### CHIP — for children

**CHIP** (Children's Health Insurance Program) covers children in families that earn **too much for Medicaid** but can't afford private insurance. It's run by the states too, often as part of the Medicaid program.

## Part 3 — Patients with both: dual eligibles

Some people have **both** Medicare and Medicaid — usually older or disabled people with low income. They are called **dual eligible** (or "duals").

The order is always the same:

1. **Medicare pays first** (primary)
2. **Medicaid pays second** (secondary), for the patient's share — the deductible and the 20% coinsurance

Medicare can often send the claim on to Medicaid **automatically** after it pays. This is called a **crossover** claim. If the crossover doesn't arrive, the biller must send the secondary claim to Medicaid manually, with Medicare's payment details.

**Be careful:** some dual eligible patients are enrolled in a program that pays their Medicare costs, called **QMB**. Providers are **not allowed** to bill a QMB patient for Medicare deductibles or coinsurance — even if Medicaid pays nothing. Sending that patient a bill breaks federal rules.

> **Technical name:** **QMB** = Qualified Medicare Beneficiary. Medicare's remittance (the 835 file) tells you when a patient is QMB. Crossovers are handled by Medicare's **Coordination of Benefits Agreement (COBA)** program. Some duals are in a **D-SNP** — a Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan, a Medicare Advantage plan built for people with both.

(For more on billing order, read [Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Insurance](/blog/primary-secondary-tertiary-insurance/).)

## How billing is different

| | **Medicare** | **Medicaid** |
|---|---|---|
| **Where the claim goes** | The MAC for your region (original Medicare) or the Advantage plan | The state Medicaid program, or the managed care plan |
| **Provider sign-up** | The provider must enroll with Medicare | The provider must enroll separately with **each state's** Medicaid |
| **Claim deadline** | 12 months from the date of service | Set by each state (and each managed care plan) |
| **Rules** | One national set of rules | Different in every state |

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Billing original Medicare for a Medicare Advantage patient.** The claim is rejected, and time is lost. Always check for a plan card and verify eligibility first.
- **Mixing up the names.** Typing "Medicare" when the patient has "Medicaid" in the software sends the claim to the wrong payer entirely.
- **Billing Medicaid first.** If the patient has any other insurance, Medicaid pays last.
- **Sending a bill to a QMB patient** for their Medicare deductible or coinsurance.
- **Checking Medicaid eligibility only once.** It can end between visits.
- **Assuming every state's Medicaid works the same.**

## The one thing to remember

**Medicare is based on age, Medicaid on income — and the card in the patient's hand decides where the claim goes.** Ask for every card at check-in, verify eligibility before each visit, and remember: Medicare (or any other insurance) pays first, Medicaid pays last.

## Words you'll hear

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Medicare** | Federal health insurance for people 65+ and some younger disabled people |
| **Medicaid** | Health coverage for people with low income, run by each state |
| **Original Medicare** | Parts A and B, paid directly by the government |
| **Part A** | Hospital insurance — inpatient stays, nursing facilities, hospice |
| **Part B** | Medical insurance — doctor visits, outpatient care, tests |
| **Medicare Advantage (Part C)** | A private plan that provides the patient's Medicare benefits instead of the government |
| **Part D** | Prescription drug coverage |
| **Medigap** | A private supplement plan that pays what original Medicare doesn't |
| **MAC** | Medicare Administrative Contractor — the company that processes Medicare claims in a region |
| **MBI** | Medicare Beneficiary Identifier — the patient's 11-character Medicare ID |
| **Medicaid managed care** | A private plan that provides a patient's Medicaid benefits |
| **CHIP** | Children's Health Insurance Program |
| **Dual eligible** | A patient with both Medicare and Medicaid |
| **Crossover** | A claim Medicare forwards automatically to the secondary payer |
| **QMB** | A dual eligible patient who can't be billed for Medicare deductibles or coinsurance |
| **Payer of last resort** | Pays only after all other insurance — Medicaid |
