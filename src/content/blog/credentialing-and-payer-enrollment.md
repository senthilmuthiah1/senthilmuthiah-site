*Part of my beginner series on US medical billing. No healthcare background needed.*

A clinic hires a new doctor. She has a medical license, an NPI and a full appointment book. She sees patients from her first Monday.

Two months later, the clinic notices that **none** of her insurance claims have been paid.

Nothing was wrong with the claims. The problem was that the insurance companies **didn't know her yet**. She had never been **credentialed** or **enrolled** with them.

This lesson explains what that means, why it takes so long, and what the billing team has to do.

## Two steps: "Is she qualified?" and "Can we pay her?"

Think of a **company travel booking system**. Before a new employee can book a flight, two things must happen:

1. **HR checks the person** — is this really an employee, with the right documents?
2. **IT sets up their account** — a login, a cost center, a start date. Until then, the booking system won't accept their trips.

An insurance company does the same with every doctor:

| Step | The question | What it involves |
|---|---|---|
| **Credentialing** | "Is this provider qualified and safe?" | Checking license, education, board certification, malpractice insurance, work history, sanctions |
| **Payer enrollment** | "Can our system pay this provider?" | A contract, a provider ID in the insurer's system, the clinic's Tax ID and address, and an **effective date** |

In everyday office talk, people often say "credentialing" for **both** steps. That's fine — just know there are two parts, and a doctor can be stuck in either one.

> **Technical name:** for Medicare, enrollment is done online in **PECOS** using the **CMS-855** forms (855I for individual practitioners, 855B for clinics and groups). Each state runs its own **Medicaid** enrollment.

## How it works, step by step

![Credentialing and payer enrollment: gather documents, the insurer verifies and contracts, enrollment gets an effective date, then the provider must be kept current with CAQH every 120 days, re-credentialing every 36 months and Medicare revalidation every 5 years](/images/posts/credentialing-enrollment.svg)

Let's say **Lee Family Clinic** hires **Dr. Sara Khan**, a family doctor, starting 1 March. The clinic wants her to see patients with **BlueHealth PPO**, **StarCare** and **Medicare**.

### Step 1 — Collect her documents

The clinic (or a credentialing specialist) gathers:

- her **NPI** and state **medical license**
- education, training and **board certification**
- **malpractice insurance** certificate
- **DEA** registration (if she prescribes controlled drugs)
- work history and references
- the clinic's details — **Tax ID**, group NPI, address, phone

### Step 2 — Fill in her CAQH profile

Most commercial insurers don't want a separate paper form from every doctor. Instead, Dr. Khan fills in one online profile, **CAQH ProView**, and gives each insurer permission to read it. (See [What is CAQH ProView?](#what-is-caqh-proview) below.)

### Step 3 — Apply to each insurer

The clinic sends an application to **each** insurer separately — BlueHealth, StarCare and Medicare. Each one has its own forms, its own queue and its own speed.

### Step 4 — The insurer checks everything

The insurer verifies the documents with the **original source** — the licensing board, the medical school, the malpractice insurer. Then a committee approves her.

This is the slow part. **60 to 120 days** is common, and some insurers take longer.

### Step 5 — Contract and provider ID

The insurer adds Dr. Khan to the clinic's contract, or signs a new one, and gives her a **provider ID** in its system. Some insurers use the NPI; others issue their own number.

### Step 6 — The effective date

The insurer tells the clinic the **effective date** — the first date of service it will pay for. This is the most important date in the whole process.

## What is CAQH ProView?

Imagine Dr. Khan applying to five insurers the old way. Five long forms, each asking for the same things — her license, her training, her malpractice insurance, every job she's had. Five sets of copies. And every time something changes, five updates.

**CAQH ProView** solves this. It's a free website where a doctor fills in her professional details **once**, and every insurer she allows can read them.

It's like a **passport**: you show the same document at every border, instead of proving who you are from scratch each time.

**Who runs it?** **CAQH** — the *Council for Affordable Quality Healthcare* — a non-profit group set up by health insurers to cut paperwork. ProView is its provider profile service. The insurers pay for it, so it's **free for doctors**.

**How it works, in four steps:**

1. **Get a CAQH ID.** Often an insurer adds the doctor to CAQH when she applies, and she receives an email with her **CAQH ID** — a number that identifies her profile. She can also register herself.
2. **Fill in the profile.** License, NPI, education, board certification, malpractice insurance, work history, practice locations, and uploaded copies of the documents.
3. **Give permission.** She chooses which insurers may read her profile — specific ones, or all participating plans. An insurer can't see it until she allows it.
4. **Attest.** She confirms the information is true and complete. Only then can the insurers use it.

When the clinic applies to BlueHealth, the application just says *"Dr. Khan's CAQH ID is …"*. BlueHealth reads the profile instead of asking for a pile of paper.

**The 120-day rule.** CAQH asks every provider to **re-attest** — log in and confirm everything is still correct — at least every **120 days**, even if nothing changed. Miss it, and insurers can't use the profile. Applications in progress can stall without anyone telling the clinic. Many clinics put this in a shared calendar, because the doctor usually forgets.

**What CAQH is not:**

- It's **not the credentialing itself.** CAQH holds the information. Each insurer still does its own checks and makes its own decision.
- It's **not used by Medicare.** Medicare has its own enrollment system, **PECOS**. A doctor needs both — CAQH for commercial insurers, PECOS for Medicare.
- **Medicaid varies by state.** Some states and Medicaid plans use CAQH; others have their own forms.

> **Technical name:** the profile used to be called the **Universal Provider Datasource (UPD)**; CAQH ProView replaced it. The CAQH ID is sometimes called the **CAQH number**.

## Why the effective date matters so much

Here's what happened with Dr. Khan:

| Insurer | Applied | Effective date | Visits before that date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medicare | 10 February | 1 March (her first day) | None — every visit is covered |
| BlueHealth | 1 February | 15 April | **Not paid** — 1 March to 14 April |
| StarCare | 20 February | — still "in process" | **Held** — waiting |

Dr. Khan saw BlueHealth patients from 1 March. But BlueHealth's effective date was **15 April**. Every BlueHealth visit in between is outside her enrollment.

- **Medicare** is more forgiving: the effective date is usually the **later** of the day the application was filed and the doctor's first day. And once approved, she can bill for up to **30 days before** that date. Had the clinic applied on 15 April instead, Medicare would pay only from 16 March — her first two weeks of visits would be lost.
- **Most commercial insurers** allow **nothing** before the effective date. Some will backdate if asked; many won't.

That's why good clinics start credentialing **90 to 120 days before** a new doctor's first day — not on her first day.

> **Technical name:** a claim for a date of service outside the provider's enrollment usually comes back with reason code **B7** — *this provider was not certified or eligible to be paid for this service on this date of service*. A claim where the insurer doesn't recognize the rendering provider at all may be rejected before processing, on the **277CA** acknowledgement.

## Enrollment statuses you'll see

For every provider and every insurer, the clinic tracks a status:

| Status | Means | Can we bill? |
|---|---|---|
| **Not started** | Nothing sent yet | No |
| **Submitted / In process** | Application with the insurer | Hold the claims |
| **Enrolled** | Approved, with an effective date | Yes — from the effective date |
| **Denied** | Insurer said no | No — patients may need another doctor |
| **Expired / Terminated** | Re-credentialing missed, or contract ended | No — from the end date |

A doctor can be **Enrolled** with BlueHealth, **Submitted** with StarCare and **Not started** with a fourth insurer, all at once. Billing has to check the **right row** for each claim.

## What billing does while a doctor is waiting

This is where clinics lose money. While enrollment is "in process":

1. **Hold the claims — don't send them.** A claim sent too early gets denied or rejected, and someone has to work it again later.
2. **Watch the timely filing clock.** Every insurer has a deadline for sending claims — sometimes only **90 days** from the visit. A long enrollment can eat into it. Keep a list of held claims by date of service.
3. **Release them the day the provider is enrolled** — but only those on or after the effective date (or within Medicare's 30-day window).
4. **Never bill her visits under another doctor's NPI** to "get paid faster." A claim must name the provider who actually gave the care. Medicare has narrow exceptions (such as **incident-to** and **locum tenens** rules), but they have strict conditions and are not a workaround for slow credentialing.
5. **Tell the front desk.** Some clinics book a new doctor's patients by insurer — for example, only Medicare and self-pay patients until BlueHealth is enrolled.

## Group and individual enrollment

A clinic is usually enrolled **as a group** (with its group NPI and Tax ID), **and** each doctor is enrolled **individually** and linked to that group.

Both must be in place. A common surprise: "We're in-network with BlueHealth!" — true for the clinic, but the **new doctor** isn't linked to the contract yet, so her claims still deny.

> **Technical name:** Medicare calls linking a doctor to a group **reassignment** of benefits — the doctor agrees that the group can bill and be paid for her services.

## It doesn't stop once she's enrolled

Credentialing has to be **kept current**. Three different clocks run at once:

| What | How often | If it's missed |
|---|---|---|
| **CAQH re-attestation** | Every **120 days** | Insurers can't read the profile; applications stall |
| **Re-credentialing** with each commercial insurer | Usually every **36 months** | The insurer can end her enrollment |
| **Medicare revalidation** | About every **5 years** (or when Medicare asks) | Medicare can stop her billing privileges |

On top of that: her **license**, **DEA** registration and **malpractice** certificate each have their own expiry dates.

And any **change** — a new address, a new location, joining a different group — usually has to be reported to each insurer. A doctor who starts working at a **second clinic location** may need that location added before claims from it are paid.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting credentialing on the doctor's first day.** Start 90–120 days earlier.
- **Assuming the group contract covers the new doctor.** Each provider must be added.
- **Sending claims before the effective date** and then appealing them one by one.
- **Forgetting held claims** until they're past timely filing.
- **Letting CAQH lapse** because nobody owns the 120-day reminder.
- **Not tracking re-credentialing dates** — the first sign is a sudden wave of denials.
- **Changing address or location** without telling the insurers.

## The one thing to remember

**A doctor can treat patients from day one — but each insurer pays her only from her enrollment effective date.** Start credentialing months before she starts, hold claims until she's enrolled, and keep the 120-day, 36-month and 5-year clocks running after that.

## Words you'll hear

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Credentialing** | The insurer checking that a provider is qualified |
| **Payer enrollment** | Setting the provider up in the insurer's system so claims can be paid |
| **Effective date** | The first date of service the insurer will pay the provider for |
| **CAQH ProView** | An online profile insurers read for credentialing information |
| **CAQH ID** | The number that identifies a provider's CAQH ProView profile |
| **Attestation** | The provider confirming their CAQH profile is still correct |
| **Re-credentialing** | The insurer checking the provider again, usually every 36 months |
| **Revalidation** | Medicare's version of re-checking enrollment, about every 5 years |
| **PECOS** | Medicare's online enrollment system |
| **Reassignment** | A doctor letting a group bill Medicare for her services |
| **Primary source verification** | Checking a credential with the organization that issued it |
| **Timely filing** | The deadline for sending a claim to an insurer |
